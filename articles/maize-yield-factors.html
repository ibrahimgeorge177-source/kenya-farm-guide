Factors That Affect Maize Yield in Kenya

Maize yield is the result of many factors working together. Farmers may use good seed and fertilizer, but the final harvest can still be affected by rainfall, soil conditions, planting time, weeds, pests, diseases and crop management.

Understanding these factors can help farmers make better decisions and interpret their harvest records more realistically.

It is also important to remember that no single factor guarantees a particular yield. Conditions differ between farms and between seasons.

1. Rainfall and Water Availability

Water availability is one of the major factors affecting maize production, particularly in rain-fed farming systems.

The amount, timing and distribution of rainfall can all matter. A season may receive substantial rainfall overall but still experience periods of moisture stress at important stages of crop development.

Excessive rainfall can also create problems such as waterlogging, erosion, nutrient movement and difficulties carrying out field operations.

Keep weather records.

Recording important rainfall or moisture events alongside farm activities can help explain why yields differ between seasons.

2. Soil Fertility

Soil provides the growing environment and nutrients required by the crop. Differences in soil fertility can therefore contribute to differences in maize performance.

Nutrient availability can be influenced by soil properties, previous cropping, organic matter, nutrient removal and fertilizer management.

Soil testing, where practical, can provide useful information for nutrient planning.

Read our maize fertilizer guide for more information.

3. Seed Variety

Different maize varieties can have different characteristics. These may include maturity period, disease tolerance, environmental adaptation and yield potential under suitable conditions.

A variety that performs well under one set of conditions may not necessarily perform the same way in another environment.

Farmers should therefore consider varieties that are appropriate for their location and production objectives.

4. Planting Time

Planting time can affect the conditions the crop experiences during different stages of growth.

In rain-fed systems, planting decisions are closely connected to the expected availability of moisture.

Planting too early under unsuitable moisture conditions can cause poor establishment, while very late planting may expose the crop to different conditions later in the season.

Local seasonal information and agricultural guidance should be considered when planning planting dates.

5. Plant Population and Spacing

The number of plants established in a field affects competition for available resources.

If plant population is too low, part of the available growing space may not be fully utilised. If it is too high for the available water, nutrients and soil conditions, competition between plants can increase.

Suitable spacing and plant population depend on factors such as maize variety, soil fertility, rainfall and production system.

6. Weed Competition

Weeds compete with maize plants for resources such as water, nutrients, light and growing space.

Competition can be particularly important while maize plants are still becoming established.

Farmers should monitor fields regularly and use an appropriate weed management strategy for their production system.

7. Pests

Insect pests can damage maize plants at different stages of development.

Damage can affect leaves, stems, roots, developing ears or grain, depending on the pest involved.

Regular field scouting can help farmers identify unusual symptoms early.

Pest management should be based on the problem actually observed and should follow appropriate agricultural guidance and product instructions where control products are used.

8. Diseases

Plant diseases can also affect maize development and grain production.

Disease pressure can vary with weather, variety, field history and other conditions.

Farmers should monitor plants for unusual symptoms and seek appropriate identification and management advice rather than assuming every damaged plant has the same cause.

9. Nutrient Management

Maize requires an adequate supply of nutrients for healthy development.

Both nutrient deficiencies and inappropriate nutrient management can affect crop performance.

Fertilizer decisions should consider soil conditions, crop requirements and locally appropriate recommendations.

Applying more fertilizer than necessary is not a guaranteed way to increase yield and can increase production costs.

10. Soil Moisture and Drainage

Maize needs suitable soil moisture, but excessive water can also create problems.

Poor drainage may contribute to waterlogging and can interfere with root development and field operations.

On the other hand, prolonged moisture shortage can limit plant growth and grain development.

Understanding the water conditions of a particular field is therefore important when interpreting crop performance.

11. Farm Management

The way a crop is managed throughout the season can influence its final performance.

Important management activities can include:

  • Land preparation
  • Timely planting
  • Correct plant population
  • Weed management
  • Nutrient management
  • Pest scouting
  • Disease monitoring
  • Water management
  • Harvest timing
  • Post-harvest handling

12. Harvest and Post-Harvest Losses

Yield can also be affected by what happens after the crop reaches maturity.

Losses can occur during harvesting, shelling, drying, transport or storage.

This is why farmers should distinguish between grain produced in the field and grain that remains available after post-harvest handling.

How the Factors Work Together

The factors affecting maize yield should not be viewed separately. They interact with one another.

For example, a farmer may use a suitable seed variety and apply fertilizer correctly, but a prolonged period of moisture stress can still affect the crop.

Similarly, adequate rainfall may not produce a good harvest if the crop experiences severe pest damage or strong weed competition.

This is why farm records are valuable. They help farmers look at the conditions surrounding a particular harvest instead of judging performance from yield alone.

Comparing Two Seasons

Suppose a farmer harvested more maize in one season than another. It may be tempting to conclude that one particular input caused the difference.

However, several things may have changed at the same time.

For example:

  • Rainfall may have been different.
  • Planting may have occurred at different times.
  • A different seed variety may have been used.
  • Weed or pest pressure may have changed.
  • Fertilizer use may have changed.
  • The area harvested may have been different.

Looking at several seasons of records can provide more useful information than drawing conclusions from one harvest alone.

Use Yield Records With Cost Records

Yield is only one part of farm performance.

A useful farm record can combine production and financial information.

For example:

Measure Example
Area 3 acres
Total harvest 2,700 kg
Yield 900 kg/acre
Total production cost KSh 45,000
Cost per acre KSh 15,000

Combining these measurements gives a more complete view of the production system.

Can Technology Help?

Digital tools can make it easier to record farm information and identify patterns across seasons.

A farmer or agricultural organisation can potentially combine information such as yield, location, weather, soil characteristics and farm practices to better understand production.

However, data should be interpreted carefully. A correlation between two factors does not automatically prove that one factor caused the other.

Final Takeaway

Maize yield is influenced by a combination of environmental, biological and management factors.

Rainfall, soil fertility, seed variety, planting time, plant population, weeds, pests, diseases and post-harvest handling can all contribute to the final result.

The most useful approach is to keep consistent records and look at several factors together. This gives farmers a stronger basis for understanding what happened during a particular season.

Remember:

No single factor guarantees a particular maize yield. Farming outcomes depend on local conditions and interactions between weather, soil, crop genetics and management.
