Intercropping Guide for Farmers in Kenya

Intercropping is the practice of growing two or more crops on the same piece of land during part or all of the same production period.

Farmers may use intercropping as part of a broader farm management strategy. The crops selected, planting arrangement, soil conditions, rainfall and management practices all influence how well an intercropping system performs.

Intercropping is not automatically better than growing a single crop. The benefits and challenges depend on the crops and the conditions under which they are grown.

What Is Intercropping?

In a monocropping system, a farmer generally grows one main crop over a particular area.

In an intercropping system, different crops share the same field. They may be planted at the same time or at different times, depending on the production system.

Common examples of combinations used by farmers can include cereals with legumes or other crops, although the most suitable combination depends on the local environment and the farmer's objectives.

Why Farmers Consider Intercropping

Farmers may consider intercropping for several reasons.

  • To produce more than one crop from the same land area.
  • To diversify farm production.
  • To produce food for household use.
  • To produce different products for different markets.
  • To spread some production risks across crops.
  • To make use of differences in crop growth patterns.

These potential benefits should be considered alongside the additional management requirements of growing multiple crops together.

Choose Crops Carefully

Crop selection is one of the most important parts of intercropping planning.

Farmers should consider how the crops interact in terms of:

  • Water requirements
  • Nutrient requirements
  • Root growth
  • Plant height
  • Canopy development
  • Growing period
  • Harvesting time
  • Market demand

Crops with very similar resource requirements may compete strongly when conditions are limited.

Intercropping Maize and Beans

Maize and beans are an example of a crop combination used in some farming systems.

The two crops have different growth characteristics, but they can still compete for water, nutrients, light and space.

Farmers considering this combination should therefore plan the arrangement rather than simply adding beans to an existing maize field without changing management.

Plant population, spacing, soil fertility, rainfall and the timing of planting can all affect the performance of the system.

Think About Crop Competition

Every crop requires resources to grow.

When crops are grown together, they may compete for:

  • Sunlight
  • Water
  • Nutrients
  • Growing space

Competition can become more important when resources are limited. For example, during a period of inadequate rainfall, crops sharing the same soil may compete more strongly for available water.

Consider Plant Height and Canopy

The physical structure of crops can influence how they interact.

A tall crop may shade a shorter crop, particularly when plant populations are high or the planting arrangement is unsuitable.

Farmers should consider how much light each crop requires and how the canopy will develop throughout the season.

Plan Spacing Carefully

Intercropping requires more careful spatial planning than simply planting one crop.

The appropriate arrangement depends on the crops being combined, varieties, field conditions and local recommendations.

Planning Factor Question to Consider
Row arrangement How should each crop be distributed across the field?
Plant population Is the combined population likely to create excessive competition?
Crop height Will one crop create excessive shade for another?
Harvest timing Can each crop be harvested without causing unnecessary damage to the other?

Consider Soil Fertility

Different crops can have different nutrient requirements.

Intercropping therefore requires farmers to think about the total nutrient demand of the system rather than considering only one crop.

Soil testing, where practical, can provide useful information for nutrient-management decisions.

Farmers should also consider previous crops and any organic or inorganic inputs already applied to the field.

Manage Water

Water availability can strongly influence intercropping performance.

In rain-fed systems, farmers should consider whether expected rainfall is sufficient for the combined crops.

Where irrigation is available, water planning should consider the requirements of all crops in the system.

Good soil management can also help improve the field's ability to retain and use available moisture.

Think About Weeds

Intercropping can change the way weeds develop and how farmers access the field for weed management.

Farmers should plan weed management before planting because some methods may be easier to use in one crop arrangement than another.

Regular field scouting helps identify weed problems early.

Monitor Pests and Diseases

Each crop in an intercropping system can have its own pest and disease risks.

Farmers should therefore monitor all crops rather than focusing only on the main crop.

When symptoms or pest damage appear, correct identification should come before choosing a control method.

Where crop protection products are used, farmers should follow product labels, safety requirements and locally appropriate agricultural guidance.

Intercropping and Farm Risk

Growing more than one crop can diversify production, but it does not eliminate farming risk.

Different crops may respond differently to the same weather conditions, pests, diseases or market changes.

Farmers should therefore evaluate the entire production system rather than assuming that intercropping automatically guarantees a better financial result.

Consider Harvesting

Harvesting can become more complicated when crops mature at different times.

Farmers should consider:

  • Which crop matures first
  • How harvesting will affect the remaining crop
  • Available labour
  • Storage requirements
  • Transport requirements
  • Market timing

Keep Separate Crop Records

Good records are particularly useful when several crops are grown on the same field.

Farmers should try to record production information separately for each crop where practical.

Record Information to Capture
Field area Total area under the intercropping system
Crop combination All crops planted together
Varieties Variety used for each crop
Planting dates Date each crop was established
Inputs Seed, fertilizer and other inputs
Labour Major activities and costs
Crop problems Pests, diseases and field observations
Harvest Quantity harvested for each crop
Sales Quantity sold and price received

Compare Intercropping With Single-Crop Production

Farmers should compare the actual results of an intercropping system with alternative production systems rather than relying on assumptions.

Useful measures include:

  • Total production
  • Production per acre
  • Total production cost
  • Revenue
  • Profit
  • Labour requirements
  • Market performance

These measurements can help farmers determine how the system fits their own farming objectives.

Understanding Farm Costs

Costs in an intercropping system can be shared between crops, making financial analysis more complicated than simply adding up individual crop budgets.

Farmers should record major expenses and, where possible, estimate how shared costs relate to each crop.

Estimating Revenue and Profit

Revenue should be considered separately for each crop before combining the results.

Total Revenue = Revenue from Crop 1 + Revenue from Crop 2 + Other Crop Revenue

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Production Costs

For example, a farmer growing maize and beans should record the quantities and selling prices of both crops rather than evaluating the field using maize revenue alone.

Advantages of Intercropping

Depending on the crops and conditions, intercropping may offer several potential advantages.

  • More than one crop can be produced from the same field.
  • Farm production can be diversified.
  • Households can obtain different food products.
  • Farmers can access more than one market.
  • Different crop growth patterns can be combined.

Potential Challenges

Intercropping also has potential disadvantages and management challenges.

  • Crops may compete for water and nutrients.
  • One crop may shade another.
  • Field operations can become more complicated.
  • Harvesting may require additional planning.
  • Some production inputs may be harder to allocate between crops.
  • Market requirements may differ between crops.

When Planning an Intercropping System

  1. Define the main objective of the farm.
  2. Choose crops that suit the local conditions.
  3. Consider water and soil availability.
  4. Plan plant populations and spacing.
  5. Consider how the crops will compete for resources.
  6. Plan weed, pest and disease management.
  7. Plan harvesting and storage.
  8. Identify markets before production where possible.
  9. Record costs and harvests separately.
  10. Compare the results with previous seasons.

A Simple Intercropping Checklist

  • Choose compatible crops.
  • Consider crop height and growth patterns.
  • Assess soil fertility.
  • Plan for available water.
  • Use suitable planting arrangements.
  • Monitor weeds, pests and diseases.
  • Plan how each crop will be harvested.
  • Record production costs.
  • Record the yield of each crop.
  • Record sales and market prices.

Final Takeaway

Intercropping can be a useful part of a diversified farming strategy, but its performance depends on how well the crops, resources and management practices fit together.

Farmers should consider competition for water, nutrients, sunlight and space while also planning for harvesting, markets and costs.

Keeping records is one of the best ways to evaluate whether a particular intercropping system is working well on a specific farm.

Important:

This article provides general educational information. Suitable crop combinations, spacing, nutrient management, planting dates and pest or disease management can vary by location, variety and farming system. Use locally appropriate agricultural guidance when making farm decisions.
