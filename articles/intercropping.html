Intercropping in Kenya

Intercropping is the practice of growing two or more crops together on the same piece of land during part or all of the growing season. This guide explains how farmers can plan crop combinations, manage competition and make better use of available land.

What is intercropping?

Intercropping involves growing different crops together rather than planting each crop separately. The crops may occupy the field at the same time or have partially overlapping growing periods.

A common example in Kenya is growing maize together with beans. Other combinations can include cereals with legumes or vegetables with compatible companion crops.

The main objective should not simply be to put more crops into the same field. Farmers need to consider crop competition, available water, soil fertility, planting dates, spacing, labour and the expected market.

Why farmers use intercropping

Better use of land

Different crops can use sunlight, soil nutrients and available growing space differently. This can help farmers make productive use of limited land.

Production diversification

Growing more than one crop can provide different products from the same field and reduce dependence on a single crop.

Food security

A field containing more than one food crop can provide households with different sources of food during the season.

Income opportunities

Different crops may be harvested or sold at different times, creating additional opportunities for farm income.

Suitable combinations depend on the local environment, farmer objectives, crop varieties, rainfall and available resources. A combination that works well in one area may not perform the same way elsewhere.

Maize and beans intercropping

Maize and beans are commonly grown together because the crops have different growth characteristics and provide farmers with two products from the same field.

Things to consider

  • Use suitable varieties for the local growing conditions.
  • Plan maize and bean spacing before planting.
  • Avoid excessive plant populations that create unnecessary competition.
  • Consider the fertility requirements of both crops.
  • Monitor weeds, pests and diseases throughout the season.
  • Plan harvesting so that one crop does not unnecessarily interfere with the other.

Choosing crops for intercropping

Good crop combinations should be selected based on the farm's conditions rather than simply following a general recommendation.

Consider the following:

  1. Water availability: Crops should have requirements that can reasonably be supported by the available rainfall or irrigation.
  2. Crop height: Very tall crops can shade shorter crops. Consider how much sunlight each crop needs.
  3. Root systems: Crops with different rooting patterns may make better use of soil resources than crops with highly similar requirements.
  4. Maturity periods: Crops with different maturity periods can sometimes make harvesting and labour management easier.
  5. Market demand: Farmers should consider whether there is a reliable market or household use for both crops.

Managing competition between crops

One of the main challenges of intercropping is competition. Crops can compete for sunlight, water, nutrients and space.

Farmers can reduce unnecessary competition by choosing appropriate crop combinations, maintaining suitable spacing and planting at appropriate times.

Field observation is important. If one crop is consistently suppressing another, the planting arrangement may need to be reconsidered in the following season.

Soil fertility and fertilizer management

Intercropped fields still require proper soil fertility management. The nutrient needs of each crop should be considered when developing a fertilizer or manure plan.

Legumes can contribute nitrogen to agricultural systems through biological nitrogen fixation, but this does not mean that every intercropped field automatically has enough nutrients for all crops.

Soil testing, where available, can help farmers make more informed nutrient management decisions.

Weed, pest and disease management

Intercropping does not remove the need for crop protection. Farmers should regularly inspect fields for weeds, insects and disease symptoms.

  • Scout the field regularly rather than waiting for severe damage.
  • Keep weeds under control, especially during early crop growth.
  • Use healthy planting material and appropriate varieties.
  • Remove or manage severely affected plants where appropriate.
  • Follow recommended pest and disease management practices.

Crop protection decisions should take into account the specific crop, pest or disease involved and the local recommendations available to farmers.

Advantages and challenges

Potential advantages

  • More than one crop can be produced from the same land.
  • Production can be diversified.
  • Farm households may obtain different food products.
  • Different crop growth patterns can make use of available resources.
  • Income sources can be diversified.

Potential challenges

  • Crops can compete for water, nutrients and sunlight.
  • Field operations may become more complicated.
  • Harvesting can require additional planning.
  • Some combinations may make pest management more difficult.
  • Not every crop combination is suitable for every farm.

Planning an intercropped field

Before planting, write down the crops you intend to grow and estimate the resources required for each one.

  1. Identify the main purpose of the field.
  2. Choose crops suited to the local conditions.
  3. Decide how much land each crop should occupy.
  4. Plan seed or planting-material requirements.
  5. Estimate fertilizer, manure and labour requirements.
  6. Plan weed and pest management.
  7. Estimate expected harvest and possible selling prices.
  8. Keep records throughout the season.

Keeping records makes it easier to compare the results of different intercropping arrangements from one season to another.

Final thoughts

Intercropping can be a useful farm management strategy, particularly where farmers want to diversify production and make productive use of limited land.

However, successful intercropping depends on the crops selected, local conditions, planting arrangement, fertility management, water availability and good field management. Farmers should evaluate their results and adjust their approach based on experience and reliable agricultural guidance.
